Looking back, I've spent most of my career trying to answer the same question:

## How do we separate signal from noise?

I've asked that question while collecting samples on remote mountain ridges, processing heavy-mineral concentrates in a laboratory, investigating geochemical anomalies, building databases, applying geostatistics, and developing machine learning workflows. The tools have changed over the years. The question hasn't.

My path into mineral exploration wasn't entirely planned.

I began my studies at the University of British Columbia in a General Biology program, drawn by an interest in how complex natural systems work. The summer before my final year, I took a full-time evening position in a heavy mineral processing laboratory. What started as just another job to support my degree quickly became something much more. I became fascinated by the process of extracting meaningful information from samples and understanding what those results revealed about the geology that produced them.

I ended up staying for seven years.

Working in the laboratory provided an education of its own. Samples arrived from exploration and mining projects across Canada, the United States, South America, Africa, Russia, Mauritania, and the Middle East. Long before I traveled widely myself, I was exposed to geological systems from around the world and the many ways geologists search for mineral deposits in very different environments.

That experience changed the direction of my career. What began as an interest in biology gradually shifted toward geology, mineral exploration, and the challenge of understanding the stories hidden within rocks, soils, sediments, plants, and geochemical signatures.

Over the next three decades, my work took me across British Columbia, Yukon, the Northwest Territories, Ontario, and Greenland. I've worked in helicopter-supported field camps, explored remote mountain ranges, processed samples in laboratories, built geospatial databases, managed exploration data, and supported programs ranging from grassroots targets to advanced-stage deposits. More recently, my work in data science and machine learning has supported exploration projects in jurisdictions around the world, including Australia, Nevada, and Africa.

Those experiences gave me a front-row seat to the entire exploration data lifecycle. I've seen how data is collected in the field, how it moves through laboratories and databases, how it is validated and interpreted, and ultimately how it influences decisions worth millions of dollars.

Along the way, I came to realize that many exploration challenges stem from both a lack of data and an underutilization of the data we already have.

Mineral exploration is fundamentally an exercise in making decisions with incomplete information. We rarely have enough drilling, enough exposure, or enough samples to eliminate uncertainty entirely.

At the same time, exploration programs often generate enormous amounts of information. Historical reports, drill results, geochemistry, geophysics, remote sensing, mapping, and field observations accumulate over decades. Much of that knowledge remains fragmented across databases, spreadsheets, reports, and corporate memory.

The challenge is not simply collecting more data. It's determining which information can be trusted, how different datasets relate to each other, and what story they collectively tell. Many important exploration decisions are limited not only by missing information, but by questions of quality, consistency, context, and interpretation.

That realization led me deeper into data management, QA/QC, geostatistics, automation, and eventually machine learning.

What makes my perspective somewhat unusual is the path I took to get here. I started in biology, built my career in geology and mineral exploration, and eventually brought those disciplines together through my PhD research on the statistical analysis and interpretation of biogeochemical data for mineral exploration.

That work reinforced something I had observed throughout my career: plants, soils, sediments, rocks, and geochemical signatures are all responding to the same underlying geological systems. Understanding those biological-geological relationships can provide powerful exploration tools, particularly in covered terrains where bedrock exposure is limited and buried mineralization can remain hidden beneath glacial deposits and vegetation.

Today, my work combines biology, geology, geochemistry, geostatistics, data quality, and machine learning.

Over the course of my career, I've been drawn to the questions that emerge when disciplines overlap:

- How do plants respond to buried mineralization?
- What patterns exist within large exploration datasets?
- How do we distinguish meaningful geological relationships from artifacts of sampling, processing, or analysis?
- How can new analytical tools help us ask better questions without losing sight of the underlying geology?

Much of my recent work has involved exploring data quality, QA/QC, geostatistics, automation, and machine learning, but the underlying motivation remains the same: understanding complex natural systems and extracting meaningful information from imperfect data.

This integrated perspective is especially relevant in regions such as central British Columbia, where extensive till cover and dense forests often obscure direct evidence of the underlying geology. As both a researcher and prospector, I remain interested in how biological and geochemical indicators can help identify buried mineral systems that traditional approaches may overlook.

Throughout my career, I've contributed to new MINFILE discoveries, authored technical and QA/QC reports, supported major exploration programs, presented research at AME Roundup, SEG, and GAC-MAC conferences, served on the boards of Women Geoscientists in Canada and the Association for Mineral Exploration, and written about the opportunities and challenges of applying AI to mineral exploration.

Exploration has changed dramatically over the course of my career. We've moved from paper maps and handwritten field notes to GIS, cloud databases, machine learning, and now AI.

Yet after three decades, I've come to realize that biology, geology, geochemistry, statistics, and machine learning are not separate disciplines.

They're different ways of observing the same natural systems.

The interesting part is figuring out how the pieces fit together.
